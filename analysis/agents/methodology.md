## 1. Single-LLM-evaluator reliability and significant-figures honesty

**What the toolkit does.** One AI evaluator, one pass, 1–10 per dimension, composites reported to two decimals (`prompts/issue-evaluator.md:113` — "Round to two decimal places"; the reference issue is branded "formula score 9.44/10", `examples/issue-calibration-set.md:102`, `README.md:77`). No instruction anywhere to average multiple runs, and no measured reliability of any kind ships with the toolkit: repo-wide grep for `test-retest|inter-rater|kappa|agreement|reproducib` returns only the unmeasured claim "The calibration set reduces scoring variance" (`examples/prompt-calibration-set.md:23`, echoed at `framework/ppep-framework.md:198`).

**What the literature says (verified).**
- Nielsen (NN/g, severity-ratings article): "Severity ratings from a single evaluator are too unreliable to be trusted"; the mean of three evaluators is "satisfactory for many practical purposes"; the mean of **four** evaluators gets within 0.5 rating point of true severity 95% of the time — i.e., ±12.5% of a 0–4 scale even with four raters. (nngroup.com is egress-blocked; wording corroborated by two independent search snippets incl. the UCalgary severity-ratings page.)
- Hertzum & Jacobsen, "The Evaluator Effect": average agreement between any two evaluators of the same system ranges **5–65%** (verified via tandfonline/Semantic Scholar search results).
- Stureborg, Alikaniotis & Suhara 2024 (arXiv 2405.01724, verified): LLM evaluators give **inconsistent scores to the same text across samples**, show round-number bias, skewed rating distributions, and sensitivity to trivial prompt differences.
- G-Eval (Liu et al., EMNLP 2023, arXiv 2303.16634, verified): even GPT-4-backed G-Eval reaches only **Spearman 0.514** with humans on SummEval, and needs probability-weighted token summation precisely because raw LLM scores cluster on a few integers.
- Scale-granularity literature (multiple sources via search — evidentlyai.com, twine.net, arXiv 2606.27226 "Ask, Don't Judge"): coarse scales (binary, 1–5) are more reliable; fine-grained scales produce "arbitrary scores"; ~0–5 is the repeatedly cited human-LLM alignment sweet spot.

**Significant-figures arithmetic (mine, shown).** The formula is a weighted sum of eight integer 0–10 scores over weights (4,4,4,3,3,3,2,2), divided by 25. Minimum nonzero increment: 2/25 = **0.08**. Propagated uncertainty if each section score independently carries ±1 point (optimistic — the rubric's own quality bands span 3 points): σ = √(3·4² + 3·3² + 2·2²)/25 = √83/25 = **±0.36**. Under a *correlated* strictness shift (one evaluator systematically harsher by δ on all sections), the overall moves by exactly δ — and the audit's Phase-3 fresh-session runs (`analysis/02-empirical-tests.md:77-84`) measured that shift at **≈1.3 points** (fresh sessions applying the shipped rubric to the anchor's own content implied ≈7.7–8.1 vs the expert's 9.44). Conclusion: **"9.44" carries two decimal places on a number whose supportable uncertainty is between ±0.4 and ±1.3; only the integer digit is signal.** Honest reporting would be integers or halves. Note also `README.md:35` claims "confidence intervals … are documented throughout"; grep proves **zero confidence intervals exist anywhere in the repo** — the phrase appears only inside the claim.

**Fair counterweight.** The audit's own Test A1 (`analysis/02-empirical-tests.md:14-37`) found run-to-run stability *better* than the literature prior: 5 fresh sessions on a novel mid-band prompt spanned 5.25–5.75 overall (±0.25), across two Claude models, and Test A2 showed the rubric moving the named dimension (Product 7→9) when named gaps were closed. The anchor mechanism plausibly does stabilize same-generation Claude scoring. Caveats: n=5/3, one day, one model family, simulated sessions, post-hoc (produced by this audit, not the owner).

## 2. Anchor calibration: set size, range coverage, single-source degradation

**Size and coverage.** Nine (prompt) and ten (issue) anchors as few-shot in-context references is defensible *prompt engineering*. As a "calibration set" in the measurement sense it lacks the defining elements used in the literature it cites: **multiple human graders and a held-out judge-vs-human agreement check**. The Label Studio post the repo cites actually describes "a small **group** of experts" grading real traces, then "testing an LLM judge against those human scores" (search-verified summary) — a validation step never performed here. LLM-Rubric (the other citation) calibrates a network **per human judge** precisely because "the humans do not fully agree with one another" (Semantic Scholar/Microsoft Research pages, verified) — it is evidence *against* single-expert ground truth, not for it.

Coverage, enumerated from the files: prompt-anchor overall scores are {1,3,3,5,7,8,9,10,10} — no anchor at 2, 4, or 6; five of nine anchors are ≥7. Per-dimension, Epistemics exemplifies only {1,1,1,6,7,8,10,10}: **the 4–5 band has zero exemplars, even though the framework's sole justification for Epistemics' special five-band scale is that "Scores 4-5 and 6-7 are distinct in this dimension"** (`framework/ppep-framework.md:134`). The mid-band 4–6 region — where the audit's A1 test put a realistic first-draft prompt (5.25–5.75) — is the thinnest-covered. Range compression at both ends: a 0.5 floor (`prompts/issue-evaluator.md:113`) and an "expert judgment override" hoisting 9.44 to the 10/10 ceiling (`examples/issue-calibration-set.md:144`).

**Single-source degradation.** All ten issue anchors are **strictly nested cumulative** degradations of one artifact (verified by reading every anchor's degradation list, `examples/issue-calibration-set.md:325,465,578,651,718,777,833,885`): score and degradation-count are perfectly confounded by construction. Consequences:
- No factorial/isolated cells — in particular **no anchor with a single absent critical section and everything else strong**, which is exactly where the formula and the band table contradict each other: (250−40)/25 = **8.4** (passes the 7.0 gate) vs the band table's "one or more critical sections absent" = **3–4 band** (`prompts/issue-evaluator.md:96-113` vs `examples/issue-calibration-set.md:133-134`). The audit's gate test B probed this cell empirically (fresh runs landed 6.24–6.68 — the gate held, but only because fresh sessions don't grant the 10s).
- Only **structural** failure modes (section removed/made vague) are represented. Content-level failure — plausible-but-wrong steps, contradictory acceptance criteria, stale file paths — never appears, yet is the failure mode that most endangers "safe to hand to an AI coding agent" (`README.md:71`).
- One domain (a hobby TCG web app), one author, one issue type, one model's output. Generalization to a second issue is untested anywhere.
- Even on its own terms the control is broken: the stated "single-change-per-anchor" principle (`:94`) is violated at step one — Anchor 2's declared change is "References removed" (`:325`) but its SQL silently drops 10 of 20 columns and an RLS policy (`:353-367` vs `:173-199`), and Context loses a paragraph (`:334-338` vs `:158`). Independently line-verified; corroborates `analysis/01-verification-log.md` item 2.

**Fairness note.** Perturbation/degradation-based construction has real precedent — e.g., AXIOM (arXiv 2512.20159, Dec 2025, title verified via search) builds LLM-judge benchmarks via "rule-based perturbation" — but with **multisource** calibration. The design instinct is sound; the claimed grounding and single-source execution are not.

**Positive finding (verified arithmetic).** The ladder is internally exact: a unique integer vector — Steps 10, AC 10, Rollback 9, Context 10, Prereq 9, Testing 9, Files 10, Refs 8 (total 236, 236/25 = 9.44) — reproduces **all** stated formula scores (8.80, 8.00, 7.28, 6.20, 5.00, 3.88, 2.76, 1.80) under the stated degradations, with every degraded value inside its rubric band (vague Testing→3, vague Rollback→2, prose AC→3, bullet Steps→4, all in "Poor 1–4"). The transparency gap: this vector is **never published** — anchors 2–10 show only aggregates, so a reader must reverse-engineer it.

**Is it derivable?** No. Nothing in the repo computes it; grep confirms the number exists only in these three assertions (one number repeated, not three corroborations). The structure is 100% − 7%, where 7% is itself asserted. Logically it is also self-undermining: the *irreducible subjectivity* of a single-evaluator method cannot be known to be 7% without exactly the multi-evaluator data the method by definition lacks.

**Do Nielsen/Hertzum support 7%?** No — they refute its magnitude. Verified: Nielsen's severity article says single-evaluator ratings are "too unreliable to be trusted" and that even the mean of four evaluators only gets within 0.5/4 = 12.5% of scale at 95% confidence. Hertzum & Jacobsen's evaluator effect: any-two-evaluator agreement 5–65% (i.e., 35–95% disagreement). Hertzum 2006 itself (search-verified; full text unfetchable — all candidate hosts egress-blocked) measured problem-prioritization/severity questions, e.g., frequency–severity correlations averaging ≈0 across nine studies and "modest" correlations among frequency/impact/persistence — it quantifies nothing at or near 7%. **Verdict: the citation is invoked for the existence of subjectivity, then a precise magnitude is invented and typeset as a measurement. It is rhetorical.** The blockquote itself is unlocatable verbatim (paraphrase-in-quotes; medium confidence given paywalls), and "Nielsen (1993)" has no entry in the framework's reference list (`:204-206` lists Nielsen 1994).

## 4. The central-tendency / controlled-degradation claim

Claim at `examples/issue-calibration-set.md:12`: controlled degradation "is grounded in NLP evaluation research which shows that synthetic low-quality examples tend to be theatrically bad rather than realistically bad, producing central tendency bias in evaluators." Cited sources (`:17-19`): LLM-Rubric, RULERS (arXiv 2601.08654), Label Studio blog.

Verified content of all three (search/fetch): **none contains this claim.** LLM-Rubric = multidimensional rubric + per-judge calibration network (no degradation, no central-tendency-from-synthetic-examples). RULERS = rubric locking, verbatim evidence extraction, Wasserstein calibration to human scores (no degradation methodology). Label Studio = expert-graded golden datasets + judge-vs-human comparison (no degradation). A web search for the distinctive phrase "theatrically bad" in evaluation contexts returns **nothing** — the phrase appears to be invented. The genuine central-tendency literature (e.g., Stureborg 2024: round-number bias, skewed score distributions) is about **judge behavior**, and would argue against this toolkit's fine-grained scale, not for its calibration-set design. Compounding this: **both** methodology citations carry wrong authors — "Eisenstein, J., et al." for LLM-Rubric (real: Hashemi, Eisner, Rosset, Van Durme, Kedzie — Semantic Scholar/MSR verified) and "Holterman, B., et al." for 2601.08654 (real: Hong, Yao, Shen, Xu, Wei, Dong; the paper is now titled "From Rubrics to Reliable Scores" — arXiv listing verified). Two invented author names on the two load-bearing citations is the signature of unverified LLM-generated citations, in a section that opens "Every claim … is traceable to a specific source" (`:8`).

## 5. Circularity and the absence of external ground truth

The full chain, documented from the repo: the owner invents PPEP → scores his own nine prompts with it ("Scores were assigned by the subject matter expert who developed the PPEP framework", `examples/prompt-calibration-set.md:12`) → embeds those scores as the evaluator's calibration → "validates" by rescoring his own prompts "until the framework stabilized" (`README.md:33`). The issue side: reference artifact produced by Claude Code on the owner's **private** repo (GitHub API verified: `Doberjohn/inkweave` exists, `"private": true`; the cited URL `…/issues/278` 404s publicly — refining earlier agents' "repo doesn't exist" to "exists but unverifiable"), scored by "the developer who owns the repository and wrote the original prompt" (`examples/issue-calibration-set.md:14`). Anchor 8, the 10/10 "gold standard" prompt, is the owner's own Inkweave prompt. The framework's central claim — the composite "predicts how useful the AI's response will be" (`framework/ppep-framework.md:19`) — is a **predictive-validity claim with zero paired observations**: no AI response quality is ever measured, once, anywhere in the repo.

Stabilization-by-self-rescoring is *convergence*, not validity. External ground truth in the toolkit: **none**. The only external inputs are citations for rubric *structure* (which sections issues should have), never for *scores*.

## 6. The five hardest interviewer questions, honest answers, and quick fixes

**Q1. "Where does 93% come from? What measurement would move it to 91%?"**
Honest answer: nowhere — it's a rhetorical acknowledgment of subjectivity formatted as a metric; no procedure exists, and the cited literature reports disagreement magnitudes (5–65% any-two agreement; single raters "too unreliable to be trusted") that dwarf 7%. *Fix (hours, do before the interview):* delete the number from all three files; replace with a qualitative limitation plus the audit's actual measured numbers (run-to-run ±0.25 on one prompt; ~1.3-point expert-vs-fresh-session offset). Saying "I removed it because I couldn't derive it" is a strong interview move; defending it is indefensible.

**Q2. "Nielsen — whom you cite — says single-evaluator severity ratings are too unreliable to be trusted and prescribes averaging 3–4 evaluators. Averaging k LLM runs costs cents. Why does your design ignore your own citation's remedy? And what is the test-retest SD behind '9.44'?"**
Honest answer: the remedy was never implemented and no reliability was measured before shipping; two-decimal reporting overstates precision by 1–2 orders of magnitude (min increment 0.08; propagated σ ≥0.36; measured systematic offset ≈1.3). *Fix (hours):* add a "score = median of 3 independent passes" instruction to both evaluators; change "round to two decimal places" to halves; run a k=5 test-retest tonight and report the SD. The audit's A1/A2 data suggests the numbers will actually look good — this gap is cheaply closable.

**Q3. "Who wrote LLM-Rubric? Who wrote RULERS? And which cited source says synthetic examples are 'theatrically bad'?"**
Honest answer: the citations name authors who did not write those papers (Hashemi et al., Hong et al. are correct), and no cited source — nor any locatable source — makes the theatrically-bad/central-tendency claim; these were evidently generated and not verified, in the very toolkit whose signature dimension is "negative claims require proof." *Fix (an hour):* correct both author lists; rewrite the grounding sentence to claim only what's true ("degradation of real artifacts is a design choice with precedent in perturbation-based judge benchmarking, e.g., AXIOM 2025; the specific central-tendency rationale was my hypothesis, not a literature finding"). This must be fixed before the interview — it is the single most checkable and most damaging item.

**Q4. "All ten anchors are nested degradations of one issue in your private repo, scored by you. How do you know the evaluator generalizes — to a second issue, another domain, or failure modes other than section-removal, like plausibly wrong steps? And your formula gives 8.4 to a perfect issue with no Rollback while your band table calls that a 3–4 — which is right?"**
Honest answer: generalization is untested; degradation count and score are confounded by construction; content-level wrongness is unrepresented; the formula/band contradiction is real arithmetic (the audit's gate test showed it doesn't bite on *this* artifact only because fresh sessions score sections below 10). *Fix (a day):* build a second mini-ladder (4–5 anchors, factorial: one isolated critical-section deletion) from a public OSS issue in a different domain; add either a cap rule ("any absent critical section caps overall at 4") or drop the band table. Both are announceable as "known limitation, fix in progress."

**Q5. "Your anchors were scored by the framework's author using the framework, and validation was rescoring your own prompts until they stabilized. Where is any independent evidence — one other rater, one held-out item, one measured response — that a 9/10 PPEP prompt yields a better answer than a 5/10 one?"**
Honest answer: nowhere yet; stabilization is self-consistency, not validity; the predictive claim has zero outcome data. The candid limitations prose partially mitigates the *tone* but not the gap. *Fix:* run the weekend-scale predictive pilot (3 prompts at different PPEP levels → blinded ranking of responses) and, if possible, get even one colleague to blind-score the nine anchors and report agreement. Presenting *any* such number — even unflattering — converts the project's central claim from assertion to preliminary evidence, which is exactly the epistemic standard the toolkit preaches (`framework/ppep-framework.md:117`: "The Epistemics dimension forces Claude to earn its conclusions rather than assert them" — an interviewer will notice the framework does not currently meet its own bar).
